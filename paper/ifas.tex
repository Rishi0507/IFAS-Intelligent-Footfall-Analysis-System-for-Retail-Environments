\begin{abstract}
Most retail stores record many hours of CCTV footage every day, yet very little of
it is ever analysed. We present IFAS, a system that processes this footage and
reports how many people visited, their gender and approximate age group, how long
they stayed and where in the frame they spent their time. Instead of one large
model for everything, IFAS uses a separate model for each task. People are
detected with YOLOv8 and followed across frames by a simple IoU-based tracker.
Gender is predicted by a ViT-B/16 network fine-tuned on the PETA dataset, while age
is predicted by four support vector machines trained on colour and texture
histograms, in the manner of the original PETA baseline. Because each person
appears in many frames, their predictions are pooled into a single label. On PETA
the gender model reached up to 97.8\% validation accuracy in short training runs,
and the age model obtained a mean accuracy of 0.835 over four age groups on a
held-out test split (76.7\% four-way accuracy), with about four in five of its
errors falling in an adjacent age group. Both splits are made at the image level,
so the same person can appear on both sides, and these figures are likely to be
optimistic. We also study how sensitive each model is to its main settings,
measure the runtime of every stage on a laptop CPU, and run the full pipeline on a
short shop recording to see where detection and tracking break down and how much
two additions, rejoining lost tracks and a second detector for weak detections,
are able to repair. On images the models never trained on, and on a lecture-room
recording labelled for the purpose, the gender model loses accuracy and the age
SVMs are at chance; averaging the gender network with CLIP used zero-shot, taking
age from CLIP, spreading each person's votes over their stay and reporting
``not sure'' below a confidence threshold fixes most of the visible errors.
Finally, we measure which seats and tables a fixed camera shows as taken, a
question that needs no identity and holds up where head counts do not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A store that wants to know how many customers came in on a given day usually has
two options: someone at the door with a hand counter, or an overhead sensor at the
entrance. Neither tells the store who the visitors were, how long they stayed or
which part of the floor they spent time in. The security cameras that are already
installed capture all of this, but in practice the recordings are rarely looked at
unless something goes wrong.

Going through the footage by hand is not realistic. A person can only watch one
screen at a time, and manual counts start to drift after a few minutes. A modest
store with eight cameras open for twelve hours generates close to a hundred hours
of video every day, so the analysis has to be automated before the footage is of
any real use.

In this paper we describe IFAS, the Intelligent Footfall Analysis System. IFAS
takes a recorded video as input and produces a record for every person who appears
in it, containing their gender, their age group, the times at which they entered
and left the frame, and the path they followed. A web dashboard reads these
records and presents them as counts, charts and a short written summary.

Our main design decision was to pick, for each sub-task, the simplest model that
does the job well. Gender is a binary label with plenty of training data
available, which makes a pretrained vision transformer~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} a
natural choice. We fine-tune ViT-B/16 in two stages, first on the public PETA
dataset~\cite{deng2014peta} and then on footage from the target store. Age is much
harder to read from a low-resolution pedestrian crop, and PETA has comparatively
few examples of older people. For age we therefore follow the recipe of the
original PETA baseline~\cite{deng2014peta} and train one support vector machine
per age group on colour and texture histograms. For detection, speed matters more
than capacity, so a small YOLOv8 model~\cite{jocher2023yolov8} is used.

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item an end-to-end pipeline that combines detection, tracking and two attribute
        classifiers, and pools several predictions per person into one label;
  \item a sensitivity study of four training settings of the gender model and
        five feature settings of the age model;
  \item an evaluation of the age model on a held-out PETA test split, including a
        look at the kinds of errors it makes;
  \item stage-by-stage timings on a laptop CPU and the per-person cost that
        follows from them; and
  \item tracker extensions that rejoin people the detector has lost and consult a
        second detector only about weak detections, along with a field check on
        shop footage showing what each change fixes;
  \item a re-evaluation on held-out identities and on labelled CCTV of a crowded
        room, which traces the visible labelling errors to their causes and
        replaces the classifiers and voting accordingly; and
  \item zone occupancy from one fixed camera: which seats and tables are taken,
        for how long, and which is likely to free up next.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews
related work and Section~\ref{sec:system} describes the system.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} presents our experiments, Section~\ref{sec:rq4}
studies zone occupancy, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the results and
their limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Pedestrian Attribute Recognition}
The PEdesTrian Attribute (PETA) dataset was introduced by Deng et
al.~\cite{deng2014peta}. It brings together ten smaller surveillance datasets into
19,000 images of 8,705 people, each annotated with 61 binary and four multi-class
attributes, gender and age group among them. Their baseline describes each image
with colour and texture features and trains a separate SVM for every attribute.
Results are reported as mean accuracy (mA), the average of the accuracy on
positive and on negative examples, so a classifier that always predicts the
majority class gets no credit for it. Most later work on pedestrian attributes uses
the same measure, and so do we. Jia et al.~\cite{jia2021rethinking} later showed
that the usual PETA partition places images of the same person in both the training
and the test set, which inflates reported results, and proposed identity-disjoint
splits to address this.

\subsection{Vision Transformers}
The transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention} was originally designed for language.
Dosovitskiy et al.~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} applied it to images by cutting an
image into $16\times16$ patches and treating them as a sequence of tokens. When
pretrained on a large dataset such as ImageNet-21k, their Vision Transformer (ViT)
matches or outperforms convolutional networks, and ViT-B/16 has since become a
common starting point for transfer learning. CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} learns an
image encoder jointly with a text encoder. A gender classifier has no use for the
text side, so we chose a vision-only backbone.

\subsection{Colour and Texture Descriptors}
Local binary patterns (LBP)~\cite{ojala2002lbp} describe texture by comparing each
pixel with a ring of neighbouring pixels. The rotation-invariant uniform variant is
unaffected by rotation and by monotonic changes in brightness, and computing it at
several radii captures texture at more than one scale. Colour histograms complement
this by describing how colours are distributed within a
region~\cite{swain1991color}. Support vector machines~\cite{cortes1995svm} work
well on fixed-length descriptors of this kind. For histograms the intersection
kernel is a popular choice, and it is the kernel used in the PETA
baseline~\cite{deng2014peta}.

\subsection{Detection, Tracking and Counting}
YOLO~\cite{redmon2016yolo} predicts all the boxes in an image in a single pass,
which made real-time detection practical. YOLOv8~\cite{jocher2023yolov8} is a
recent member of the family and includes small models that run on modest hardware.
Counting people by detecting them has the advantage of being easy to audit, since
every count is backed by a box. Density-map methods estimate crowd size without
locating individuals and are better suited to dense crowds, but a store entrance
seldom holds more than a few dozen people at once, so detection is enough here.
Tracking by detection links boxes across frames, and SORT~\cite{bewley2016sort}
showed that even a simple association step works well when the detector is
reliable.

\subsection{Transfer Learning}
A pretrained network is usually adapted to a new task either by linear probing,
where only a new output layer is trained on frozen features, or by full
fine-tuning, where every weight is updated. Kumar et
al.~\cite{kumar2022finetuning} showed that fine-tuning with a randomly initialised
head can distort the pretrained features, and that probing first avoids much of
this damage. We use this two-phase schedule together with
AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} and a cosine learning rate schedule with
warmup~\cite{loshchilov2017sgdr}.

\section{System Design}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Overview}
The overall pipeline is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, and
Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} lists the steps in more detail. The video is read
frame by frame, a detector finds the people in each frame, and a tracker links the
detections that belong to the same person. Crops of every tracked person are
passed to the gender and age classifiers, and their outputs are pooled per person.
The result is saved as one JSON record per video, which is what the dashboard
reads.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Processing one video}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require frames $f_0, f_1, \dots$ at $r$ frames per second; stride $s$; IoU threshold $\tau$; vote gap $g$; vote cap $m$
\Ensure one record per person
\State $A \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{open tracks}
\For{every $s$-th frame $f_k$}
  \State $B \gets$ person boxes in $f_k$ with confidence $\geq 0.10$
  \State $P \gets$ pairs of a track in $A$ and a box in $B$ with IoU $\geq \tau$
  \For{each pair $(t,b)$ in $P$, highest IoU first}
    \If{$t$ and $b$ are both unmatched}
      \State extend $t$ with $b$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State drop unmatched boxes that lie mostly on a matched person
  \State rejoin an unmatched box to a recently lost track near it
  \State start a track for each remaining box, tentative if below $0.4$
  \State ask the second detector about tentative tracks that persist
  \State close each track unmatched for more than $r$ sampled frames
  \For{each track $t$ matched in $f_k$}
    \If{$t$ is confirmed, has $n < m$ votes, its last vote is at least $g\,2^{\lfloor n/4 \rfloor}$ old (capped) and its box overlaps no other by IoU $> 0.3$}
      \State crop $t$ from $f_k$ and classify gender and age
      \State add both probability vectors to the votes of $t$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State discard unconfirmed tracks and tracks seen in fewer than three frames
\State \Return label, confidence, dwell time and path of each track
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Detection and Tracking}
\label{sec:tracking}
Detection uses YOLOv8n restricted to the person class. A box needs a confidence of
at least 0.4 to start a new track, but weaker boxes down to 0.10 are also kept and
are handled as described in Section~\ref{sec:hunches}. Boxes smaller than
$8\times16$ pixels are thrown away because they hold too little detail to
classify, and detection is run on every second frame, which halves its cost.

Tracking is done by greedy matching on intersection over union (IoU), that is, the
area two boxes share divided by the total area they cover. Every pair of an open
track and a new box with an IoU of 0.3 or more is a candidate. Candidates are
taken from the highest IoU downwards, and a pair is accepted only if neither the
track nor the box has been matched already. A track is closed once it has gone
unmatched for as many sampled frames as the video has frames per second, which
works out to two seconds at the default stride. Tracks seen in fewer than three
frames are assumed to be detector noise and are dropped.

Two further rules stop one person from being counted several times. The first
discards a box when half or more of it lies on a person who has already been
matched in that frame. This removes double detections, for example a person and
their reflection in a glass panel. The second rule rejoins people who have been
lost. People rarely leave the scene from the middle of the frame, so a track that
ends there has most likely been dropped by the detector. If a box with a
confidence of at least 0.25 then appears within one body height of that spot
within the next 8\,s, it continues the old track. Near the edges of the frame,
where people really do leave and arrive, a box rejoins a track only if the earlier
person was standing still and the new box is in the same place.

In crowded, seated rooms the people at the back are small to a 640-pixel
detector. There the detector also looks at a $2\times2$ grid of overlapping tiles,
and boxes are merged by non-maximum suppression~\cite{akyon2022sahi}. Seated
neighbours' boxes then overlap enough to be confused, so in this mode a box must
overlap a person's last box by an IoU of 0.5 to continue them
(Section~\ref{sec:cctv}).

The tracker has no appearance model. This keeps it cheap, but it also has clear
weaknesses, which are listed in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{Weak Detections and the Second Detector}
\label{sec:hunches}
As mentioned above, the detector runs with a confidence floor of 0.10, well below
the 0.4 needed to start a track. Boxes that fall in between are used in two ways.
A weak box that overlaps someone who is already being tracked simply extends that
track, since a weak detection on a known person is usually genuine. A weak box
anywhere else opens a tentative track, which is followed like any other track but
is not counted.

A tentative track can become a person in two ways. If a later box on the same
track reaches 0.4, the track is confirmed. Otherwise, once the track has collected
eight detections, a second and larger detector (YOLOv8m) is run on the same frame
at input sizes of 640 and 960 pixels, and the track is confirmed if it finds a
person on that spot with a confidence of 0.25 or more. This check is made at most
four times per track and no more than once a second. A track that is never
confirmed is discarded, and its location is remembered so that the same object is
not checked again.

Once confirmed, a track keeps its full history, so the dwell time of that person
is counted from the first weak sighting. If the track started where a known person
was recently lost, the two tracks are merged.

The main reason for this arrangement is cost. As Section~\ref{sec:field} shows,
the larger model finds people that the small one misses but is roughly six to
eleven times slower. Running it only on tentative tracks gives most of its benefit
for a small fraction of the cost.

\subsection{Gender Classifier}
\label{sec:gender}
Each crop is resized to $224\times224$ pixels and normalised with the ImageNet mean
and standard deviation. The backbone is the public ViT-B/16 checkpoint
\texttt{google/vit-base-patch16-224}, which has 86 million parameters and was
pretrained on ImageNet-21k and then fine-tuned on ImageNet-1k. It splits the crop
into 196 patches, adds a class token and passes the sequence through 12 encoder
layers. A pooling layer maps the class token to a 768-dimensional vector, and a
single linear layer with 1,538 parameters maps that vector to two logits, which a
softmax turns into probabilities (Fig.~\ref{fig:models}a). The loss is
cross-entropy.

One detail of this setup deserves mention. The public checkpoint does not include
weights for the pooling layer, so that layer starts from random values. In our code
it belongs to the backbone and so stays frozen during the linear probe described
below, which means the probe is trained on a random projection of the class token.
This may partly explain why the probe on its own stops at about 0.79 accuracy
(Section~\ref{sec:gender-exp}).

Training runs in two stages, each with two phases (Table~\ref{tab:training}). In
Phase~A, the linear probe, the backbone is frozen and only the new head is trained,
with a learning rate of \sci{-3}. In Phase~B the backbone is unfrozen and the whole
network is trained with a learning rate of \sci{-5}. Stage~1 runs both phases on
PETA. Stage~2 repeats them on labelled crops from the target store, with both
learning rates divided by ten so that the model can adapt to the store without
forgetting what it learned from PETA. Stage~2 is part of the training code, but we
do not evaluate it in this paper.

In the current pipeline the network's probabilities are averaged with those of
CLIP ViT-B/32~\cite{radford2021clip} used zero-shot, which compares a crop's
embedding with the embeddings of a few prompts such as ``a CCTV image of a man''
and ``a CCTV image of a woman''. CLIP needs no training and is strongest on large
CCTV crops, where the fine-tuned network is weakest (Section~\ref{sec:cctv}).

Within each phase the learning rate rises linearly from zero over the first part
of the phase, a fraction set by the warmup ratio, and then decays to zero along a
cosine curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:schedule}). All runs use
AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} with a batch size of 64, mixed precision and
gradient clipping at 1.0. Training images are flipped horizontally with
probability 0.5 and their brightness, contrast and saturation are jittered
slightly. One tenth of the data is held out for validation, using a split
stratified by gender.

\subsection{Age Classifier}
The age model does not use a neural network at all. Each crop is cut into $R$
horizontal strips of equal height; with four strips these correspond roughly to
the head, the torso, the upper legs and the lower legs. Two kinds of histogram are
computed for every strip. For colour there is one histogram of $B$ bins for each of
the red, green, blue, hue and saturation channels. For texture there is one
histogram of rotation-invariant uniform LBP codes for each radius in a set
$\mathcal{S}$, computed with $Q$ sampling points, which gives $Q+2$ bins per
radius. All histograms are L1-normalised and the histograms of all strips are
concatenated into one feature vector (Fig.~\ref{fig:models}b) of length
\begin{equation}
  d = R\,\bigl(5B + |\mathcal{S}|\,(Q+2)\bigr).
  \label{eq:dim}
\end{equation}

We train one SVM for each of the four age groups, 16 to 30, 31 to 45, 46 to 60 and
above 60. Each one is a binary classifier that separates its own group from the
other three. For every group we try two kernels: the histogram intersection kernel
\begin{equation}
  K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{i}\min(x_i, y_i),
\end{equation}
and the standard radial basis function (RBF) kernel with width parameter $\gamma$.
The penalty $C$ is searched over $\{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100\}$ for both kernels and
$\gamma$ over $\{0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1\}$ for the RBF kernel, which gives 25 settings
per group, and the one with the highest validation mean accuracy is kept. For a
single attribute, mean accuracy is
\begin{equation}
  \mA = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mathit{TP}}{P} + \frac{\mathit{TN}}{N}\right),
  \label{eq:ma}
\end{equation}
where $P$ and $N$ are the numbers of positive and negative examples and
$\mathit{TP}$ and $\mathit{TN}$ are how many of each are classified correctly. At
test time a crop is given the age group whose SVM returns the largest decision
value.

PETA also has a label for people under 15, but there are too few such images to
train a stable classifier, and our early models underfitted this group every time.
We therefore left it out.

On identities they never saw, the SVMs turn out to be at chance
(Section~\ref{sec:cctv}). The pipeline therefore takes age from CLIP zero-shot by
default, with one prompt set per group (a young person or a student, an adult, a
middle-aged person, an old person), and keeps the SVMs as an option.

\subsection{Pooling Predictions per Person}
\label{sec:pooling}
Any single crop may be blurred or partly hidden, but a person is usually visible
for many frames, so IFAS classifies each track several times and combines the
answers. A track is first classified at most once every 0.4\,s, and the gap
doubles after every four votes, up to 45\,s, with at most 24 votes in total. The
votes thus cover the whole stay rather than its first seconds, and the cap
limits the cost of someone who stays in view for several minutes. In the
original design the gap stayed at 0.4\,s with at most 12 votes, so all the votes
of a long stay came from its first five seconds. A crop that overlaps another
person's box by an IoU above 0.3 is not classified at all, since it shows two
people.

Each classified crop votes for the label it finds most probable, and the track
receives the label with the most votes, with ties going to the label with the
higher total probability. The confidence we report for a person is simply the
average probability that the crops gave to the winning label. For the age model,
the SVM decision values are first turned into a distribution with a softmax. This
puts age confidence in the same 0 to 1 range as gender confidence, but the two are
not calibrated against each other and should not be compared directly.

A label is only reported when it can be trusted. Below a pooled confidence of 0.6
for gender or 0.4 for age the person is reported as ``not sure'', and so is a
person whose votes in the first and second halves of the stay confidently name
different labels, which happens when a box passes from one person to another.
The annotated video draws each box with the label of its latest votes, not of
the whole stay.

\subsection{Analytics and Dashboard}
For every track IFAS records when the person was first and last seen and the dwell
time in between, and sorts the dwell time into one of the five tiers in
Table~\ref{tab:tiers}. The video is also divided into 5\,s bins, and for each bin
the record stores the number of arrivals and exits along with the mean and peak
number of people in view. A grid of $16\times9$ cells laid over the frame
accumulates where people stood, and the frame is further divided into three
vertical zones.

\caption{Dwell tiers}
\label{tab:tiers}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Tier & Time in frame & Reading \\
\midrule
Passing  & under 3\,s    & walked through \\
Glancing & 3 to 10\,s    & slowed or looked \\

Since recordings vary in length, IFAS compares them by rate rather than by raw
count. Each recording is split by the clock hours it covers, and for every hour we
store the number of arrivals and the number of minutes that were actually watched.
The arrival rate for any group of hours, such as all Saturdays, is the total
number of arrivals in those hours divided by the total minutes watched, expressed
per hour. This way a three-minute clip cannot outweigh a three-hour export. A day
is only included if it has at least five minutes of footage, and a trend is drawn
only once three such days are available.

The dashboard shows the results at two levels of detail. The simple view, meant for
the store manager, summarises the findings in plain sentences alongside a few
headline charts. The detailed view adds filters by gender, age group, dwell tier
and confidence, a table of every tracked person and the confidence distributions
of both models. A separate guide explains each recording in plain words for the
person who runs the place: how busy it was, when people arrived, whether they
stopped, where they went, how the seats were used and who visited, each with
suggestions, the numbers behind them and how far they can be trusted. A zones view
shows the seats and tables of Section~\ref{sec:rq4} on the camera picture.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}
All experiments use PETA~\cite{deng2014peta}. For the gender model we use every
image that has a gender label, about 19,000 in all, and hold out one tenth for
validation.

The age model uses the 14,241 images labelled with one of the four age groups we
keep. They come from nine of PETA's ten subsets: the CUHK subset labels each image
by its file name rather than by person, and our loader did not read it, so its
4,563 images are not included. Fig.~\ref{fig:agedata} shows how they are distributed: the two youngest
groups hold 79\% of the images and the oldest only 8\%. We split them into
training, validation and test sets in the same 9,500\,:\,1,900\,:\,7,600
proportions as the PETA protocol, giving 7,120 training, 1,424 validation and
5,697 test images, stratified by age group.

Both splits are made at the image level. PETA contains several images of many of
its 8,705 people, so the same person can end up in the training data and in the
validation or test data. This is the same problem Jia et
al.~\cite{jia2021rethinking} found in the standard PETA partition. We measured
how large it is: in an image-level split with these proportions, about three in
four test images from the nine subsets that identify people (74 to 75\% over five
random splits) show a person who also appears in the training set. The
accuracies reported in this section are therefore likely to be higher than what
the models would achieve on people they have never seen, such as new customers in
a store.

should be treated as noise.

\textit{Learning rate.} With a backbone learning rate of \sci{-7} the model hardly
changes, and validation accuracy stays around 0.80, roughly what the linear probe
reaches on its own. Rates of \sci{-4} and \sci{-5} both converge, reaching 0.992
and 0.983 after four epochs of fine-tuning (Fig.~\ref{fig:gender}a). The higher
rate did a little better in this short run, but we chose \sci{-5} because a smaller
step is less likely to damage the pretrained features over the longer schedule of
the final model. This is a judgement on our part rather than something the
experiment shows. These runs also fine-tuned for one epoch longer than the probing
and weight decay runs, so their numbers cannot be compared directly with the other
panels.

\textit{Linear probe.} Skipping the probe gives the lowest accuracy, 96.8\%. Any
amount of probing helps, and four epochs gave the best result at 97.8\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gender}b). The trend is not monotonic, since two epochs scored
lower than one, which we put down to run-to-run noise. Interestingly, the training
loss tells the opposite story: over the first 200 steps of fine-tuning, the loss
falls further without a probe than with one. Lower training loss together with
lower validation accuracy is what we would expect if the network were fitting the
training set at the cost of its pretrained features~\cite{kumar2022finetuning}.

\textit{Weight decay.} Accuracy was lowest without weight decay (97.39\%) and
highest at a decay of 0.3 (97.71\%), and the gap between training and validation
accuracy was also smallest at 0.3, at 1.67 points compared with 2.14 points with no
decay (Fig.~\ref{fig:gender}c and~d). However, the whole range across the seven
values is only about a third of a point, which is within the noise of a single
run. The study does not really separate these values, and we kept 0.3 mainly
because it came out on top in both measures.

\textit{Warmup.} Training without warmup gave the lowest accuracy, 96.86\%. A
ratio of 0.2 gave the highest at 97.68\%, with 0.05 close behind at 97.61\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gender}e). The non-zero ratios follow no clear order.

Overall, the probe changes accuracy by 1.0 point across its range, warmup by 0.8
and weight decay by 0.3. Only the first two are clearly larger than the noise
level. We should also be clear about where the 97.8\% figure in the abstract comes
from. It is the best run of the probe study (four probe epochs followed by three
epochs of fine-tuning), not a run of the full schedule in Table~\ref{tab:training}.
It was also picked on the same validation set it is reported on, and it is
affected by the identity overlap described in Section~\ref{sec:data}, so it is
best read as an upper estimate.

\subsection{Runtime}
\label{sec:runtime}
We timed each stage on a laptop with a two-core AMD Ryzen~3 3250U processor and no
dedicated graphics card. The input was an $848\times478$ recording, and the
classifiers were timed on the person crops the detector found in it. Median times
are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:runtime}. Detection takes \tDetect\,ms per frame. The
age model needs \tAge\,ms per crop, of which \tAgeFeat\,ms goes into computing the
histograms. The gender network takes \tGender\,ms per crop in batches of 16 and
\tGenderOne\,ms for a single crop, and it accounts for most of the total cost,
which is why the number of votes is capped. With the original cap of 12 votes, the two
classifiers cost under \tPerson\,s per person on this machine however long the
person stays. Without the cap, a person would be classified up to two and a half
times for every second they spend in view, so a customer who stayed for a minute
would need about 150 classifications and nearly two and a half minutes of
computation. On a processor of this class IFAS is therefore a tool for recorded
footage, and a live feed would need faster hardware.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Staged Transfer Learning}
Our probing result is in line with Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2022finetuning}. A newly
added head starts from random weights, so its first gradients are large and carry
little useful information, and if the backbone is trainable at that point these
gradients flow straight into it. Training the head alone for a few epochs first
avoids the problem at little extra cost. In our case the randomly initialised
pooling layer described in Section~\ref{sec:gender} adds a second set of random
weights, and moving it into the head, or reading the class token directly, is
worth testing.

The weight decay of 0.3 that came out best is higher than the 0.01 to 0.1 usually
used with AdamW. With about 19,000 images and 86 million parameters, it is
plausible that stronger regularisation helps, but as noted in
Section~\ref{sec:gender-exp} the measured differences are within single-run noise.
Repeating the study with several random seeds is the first thing we would do with
more compute.

\subsection{Reading the Age Results}
The oldest group is the easiest on every measure. Grey or white hair changes the
colour histogram of the top region, and posture or clothing may also contribute,
although this is only our interpretation and we did not test which features are
actually responsible.

The two youngest groups are confused with each other more than any other pair,
which is not very surprising, since a 29-year-old and a 32-year-old fall on
opposite sides of a boundary but look much the same at surveillance resolution.
The confusion matrix supports the view that most errors are boundary effects: four
errors in five are off by a single group, and errors of two or three groups make up
only 4.8\% of the test set. For a store that wants a rough age profile of its
visitors, being one group out matters far less than being three groups out. Mean
accuracy does not reward this, whereas a measure that respects the order of the
groups would.

Even though the oldest group has only 8\% of the data, it reaches a recall of 0.85,
which suggests that the balanced class weights are doing their job of pushing each
SVM to find its positive examples.

Leaving out the under-15 group points to a wider problem. Surveillance datasets
contain few children, partly because few appear in such footage and partly
because privacy rules limit how this kind of data can be collected. A store that
needs to know how many children visit should not rely on IFAS for it.

Finally, although our age model follows the recipe of the PETA baseline, its
numbers cannot be set directly against those of Deng et al.~\cite{deng2014peta}.
We use our own image-level split of a subset of PETA, we drop one age group, and
the grid search chose the RBF kernel instead of the intersection kernel. A proper
comparison would need the original partition and the intersection kernel.

\subsection{Feature Settings and the Released Model}
The released model and the selected settings differ in the number of regions and
the number of LBP points. On validation data their mean $\mA$ differs by only
0.002, which is too small to separate them on a single split. The screening study
is still useful, because it shows that the model is not fragile. Across all the
settings we tried, average $\mA$ stays between 0.7155 and 0.7493, a spread of 3.4
points, and the two choices that hurt most are too few body regions and too many
colour bins.

\subsection{Why Two Kinds of Model}
A single network predicting both gender and age would be simpler to deploy, but we
kept the two separate for two reasons. The age model follows a published baseline,
and its features can be inspected directly, since each histogram bin is a colour
range in a named part of the body. The gender task, on the other hand, has enough
data to train a large network, and the accuracy of the transformer on it is hard to
match with hand-crafted features.

The runtime measurements give a third reason. Per crop, the age model costs less
than a tenth of the gender network on a CPU, so a store without a graphics card can
afford to run age on every crop and spend most of its compute budget on gender.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
Apart from the field check and the lecture-room recording, every number in this
paper comes from PETA. The field check is a single 15\,s clip annotated by eye,
and the lecture-room labels come from an AI annotator and still have to be
confirmed by people. Accuracy in a real store therefore remains unmeasured.
Several other limitations follow from the design.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Our PETA splits are made at the image level, so some people appear on
        both sides. The reported accuracies are likely to be optimistic for
        unseen customers, and an identity-disjoint split such as that of Jia et
        al.~\cite{jia2021rethinking} should be used in future work.
  \item The training results of the gender model are validation accuracies from
        short single runs, with the best one chosen on the same validation set.
        Its held-out results (Section~\ref{sec:cctv}) cover CUHK and one
        recording, and Stage~2 has not been evaluated.
  \item The tracker does not re-identify people by appearance. Someone who leaves
        the frame and comes back is counted twice, and two people who cross paths
        can swap tracks. The rejoining rule is a heuristic and can merge two people
        who stand in the same spot a few seconds apart.
  \item A person whom no detector finds is never counted. In the field check, one
        of the five people, hidden behind another, was missed for the entire clip.
  \item The tracking rules were tuned on a single recording and still need to be
        checked on labelled footage from other stores.
  \item Occupancy is the number of people in view, not the number in the store,
        and dwell time measures presence in the frame, which is only a proxy for
        interest.
  \item The age SVMs were trained at PETA resolution, so small or blurred crops
        receive low-confidence labels, and age confidence is not calibrated.
  \item Per-track accuracy has been measured on one recording only, against AI
        labels. Pooling helps most when the errors on different frames are
        independent; spreading the votes over the stay reduces, but does not
        remove, the correlation between crops of one person.
  \item The runtime figures come from one processor and one recording.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this paper we presented IFAS, a system that turns store footage into a record of
who came in, when they came and how long they stayed. It combines models chosen
task by task: a small detector for speed, a fine-tuned transformer for gender and
an SVM baseline for age. On PETA the gender model reaches up to 97.8\% validation
accuracy, and the age model achieves a mean $\mA$ of 0.835 on the test split, with
95\% of its predictions within one age group of the truth, although both figures
come from image-level splits and are probably optimistic. The sensitivity study
shows that linear probing before fine-tuning has the largest effect on the gender
model, and that the number of body regions matters most for the age model. The
timings show that the gender network dominates the cost and that capping the votes
per person keeps that cost bounded. On a difficult clip from outside PETA,
rejoining lost tracks and checking weak detections with a second detector raised
the number of people found from three of five to four of five and brought the
reported dwell times in line with the footage, although the rules were tuned on
that same clip. On images the models never trained on and on a labelled
lecture-room recording, the picture changes: the gender network loses accuracy,
the age SVMs are at chance, and the errors visible in the video trace back to
votes taken in a person's first seconds, tracks passing between seated
neighbours and crops showing two people. Averaging the network with CLIP used
zero-shot, taking age from CLIP, spreading the votes and saying ``not sure''
below a confidence threshold address each of these. Zone occupancy, which needs
no identity, stays consistent with the people visible even where head counts are
inflated five-fold.

We see three directions for future work. The first is to label footage from real
stores with two human annotators and measure per-track accuracy and seat
occupancy on it, using identity-disjoint splits for any retraining. The second is to add re-identification so that returning visitors
are counted only once. The third is to run the pipeline on a live camera feed,
which we have not tested yet.
